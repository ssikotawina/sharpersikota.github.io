About

Background

I am a PhD student in Health Economics at the University of Colorado Denver and a Fulbright Scholar. My academic training spans economics and international public health — a foundation that positions me at the intersection of rigorous empirical methods and policy-relevant health systems research. Before beginning my PhD, I worked for over a decade as a lecturer, health economist, and research consultant across Sub-Saharan Africa on projects funded by USAID, UNITAID, the Global Fund, and the UK Foreign Commonwealth and Development Office. These experiences ground my research in the practical realities of health systems in low-income countries and continue to shape my research agenda.

Research Focus

I am developing as a health services researcher focused on evaluating how health systems, infrastructure investments, and financial protection policies affect access to care, healthcare utilization, and health outcomes — particularly in Zambia and Sub-Saharan Africa. My research is organized around one central question: How can health systems and public policies reduce health inequalities and improve outcomes for vulnerable populations in resource-constrained settings?

This research agenda is organized around three connected areas: early-life health and child nutrition, examining how birth conditions shape long-term health trajectories and whether postnatal interventions can interrupt cycles of undernutrition; health systems and infrastructure, evaluating whether expansions in piped water access produce measurable gains in child health outcomes; and health insurance and financial protection, assessing whether Zambia's National Health Insurance Management Authority (NHIMA) accreditation improves healthcare utilization and reduces out-of-pocket expenditure burdens.

I am becoming a health services researcher specializing in the evaluation of health systems, infrastructure, and financial protection policies using causal inference methods — with a long-term commitment to producing evidence that is directly relevant to policy and practice in Sub-Saharan Africa.

Methods

I specialize in causal inference using quasi-experimental designs, applying regression discontinuity and difference-in-differences methods to large-scale Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data and administrative datasets. I am developing expertise in health policy evaluation and econometric methods for health services research through coursework, seminars, and dissertation work at CU Denver's Anschutz Medical Campus.

Collaboration

I am a strong collaborator, bringing experience in interdisciplinary research, program evaluation, and working with large-scale health data across multiple country contexts in Sub-Saharan Africa. I have served as health economist, evaluation team lead, and data analyst on multi-country studies — experience that has strengthened my ability to work across disciplines, institutions, and sectors. My work is directly relevant to organizations such as the World Bank, WHO, USAID, NIH Fogarty International Center, the Global Fund, and Ministries of Health, as well as academic institutions focused on global health and development economics.

Future Goals

My future research will focus on strengthening health systems in Sub-Saharan Africa through the evaluation of insurance systems, service delivery reforms, and early-life interventions — contributing to institutions at the intersection of academic research and applied health policy. I am actively developing expertise in administrative data linkage, machine learning applications for causal inference, health technology assessment (HTA), and academic grant writing for NIH and Fogarty International Center. My long-term goal is to produce research that directly informs health system strengthening and financial protection policy in resource-constrained settings, and to mentor the next generation of health economists working on low- and middle-income country health systems.

Research

My research evaluates health systems, infrastructure, and financial protection policies to understand their causal impact on health outcomes and inequalities in low- and middle-income countries. I use quasi-experimental designs applied to large-scale survey and administrative data.

Working Papers & Research in Progress

Unequal Beginnings: Do Postnatal Interventions for Low-Birth-Weight Infants Reduce Child Stunting?

Evidence from a Fuzzy Regression Discontinuity Design · Zambia DHS 2018

This paper uses the 2500g birthweight threshold as the basis for a fuzzy regression discontinuity design to estimate the causal effect of postnatal interventions on child stunting in Zambia. The study addresses measurement challenges including birthweight heaping, misclassification, and sorting around the cutoff, contributing policy-relevant evidence on the effectiveness of targeted early-life interventions in low-resource settings.

Fuzzy RD Design Child Nutrition Zambia DHS 2018 Low Birth Weight

Does Access to Piped Water Matter for Stunting Reduction in Rural Zambia?

Evidence from Zambia DHS 2007 and 2018

This project applies a difference-in-differences approach using two rounds of Zambia DHS data to evaluate whether expanded piped water access reduces child stunting in rural communities. The study contributes to the evidence base on WASH infrastructure and population health, with direct relevance for rural development and nutrition policy in Zambia.

Difference-in-Differences WASH Policy DHS Panel Data Rural Health

NHIMA Accreditation and Healthcare Utilization in Zambia

Administrative & Facility-Level Data · Health Systems Strengthening

This study uses administrative and facility-level data to assess whether accreditation under Zambia's National Health Insurance Management Authority improves healthcare utilization and service quality. The paper contributes to the literature on health insurance expansion in LMICs and provides evidence on whether accreditation mechanisms can strengthen health system performance and financial protection.
